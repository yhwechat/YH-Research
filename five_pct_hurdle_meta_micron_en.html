The 5% Hurdle: Meta Is Borrowing, Micron Is Repaying

When a Treasury held to maturity pays 5%,
even a great business has to earn its price.

In Brief. For the first time since 2007, the 10-year Treasury yields more than 5%, and every stock now has a higher bar to clear. Two companies are meeting that moment in opposite ways. Meta, one of the most profitable businesses on earth, is borrowing heavily. Micron, which lost billions three years ago, is paying its debt down early. Which is better placed is not as obvious as it looks.

Two weeks ago, ahead of the Fed's September meeting, we argued that the direction of the 10-year yield mattered more than the rate decision itself. The Fed went on to raise rates by a quarter point on September 16, a unanimous 12–0 vote and its first hike since July 2023. The 10-year has since climbed from 4.95% to 5.17% at Friday's close, its highest level since 2007. September payrolls arrive this Friday and will shape expectations for another hike on October 28.

1. What "5%" actually means

It is tempting to hear "5%" as money for nothing. It isn't quite that, and the fine print matters.

It assumes you hold to maturity. Buy a 10-year note today, keep it for the full ten years, and you lock in roughly 5% a year with no default risk. Sell early and you take whatever the market offers: each further one-point rise in yields knocks roughly 7–8% off the price of a new 10-year note.

It isn't what cash earns. One-year Treasury bills pay about 4.5%, the Fed's policy rate sits at 3.75–4%, and dollar deposits at most banks pay less than either.

It is before inflation. Strip out expected inflation and the real 10-year yield is closer to 2.85%. Investors who think in another currency also carry exchange-rate risk.

Even so, the 10-year is the right benchmark for stocks. Owning shares means owning a slice of a business for the long haul, and the honest comparison is what the same money would earn if locked away for a similar stretch with no business risk at all. On FactSet's numbers, the S&P 500 trades at about 19 times next year's earnings, an earnings yield of roughly 5.2%. The market as a whole is running level with Treasuries. Individual companies are another matter.

2. Meta: a cash machine that has started borrowing

Meta's second quarter brought in $60.8 billion of revenue, up 28%, and $31.9 billion of operating cash. Almost every dollar of that cash went straight back out: capital spending and finance-lease payments came to about $31.1 billion. Free cash flow was $0.8 billion, against $8.5 billion a year earlier.

None of this reflects a weaker business. Some 3.6 billion people use Meta's apps every day, ad impressions grew 14% in the quarter and the average price per ad rose 12%. What has changed is not how Meta makes money but how it spends it.

To bridge the gap, Meta has turned to the bond market. A $30 billion deal in 2025 was followed by another $25 billion in April, including notes that run to 2066 at a 6.45% coupon. Long-term debt went from $58.7 billion at the start of the year to $83.7 billion by the end of June. Buybacks have quietly disappeared: Meta repurchased no stock in the first half of 2026, compared with $22.9 billion a year earlier. Money that used to flow back to shareholders is now pouring into data centers.

At roughly 28 times trailing earnings, Meta offers shareholders an earnings yield of about 3.5%. That is less than a 10-year Treasury, and well under the 6.45% Meta pays the lenders who have trusted it with money until 2066. Shareholders are accepting a lower yield and a place behind the creditors. The only thing that makes that worthwhile is growth.

This month, the growth story acquired a name. On September 8 Meta launched Muse, a personal AI agent designed to get things done rather than just answer questions: it can shop, book tickets and manage a calendar. The basic version is free, with paid tiers at $20 and $100 a month. Third-party app data put Muse at 1.8 million iPhone downloads in North America in under two weeks, ahead of ChatGPT's 1.3 million over the same span at launch. Meta has also lined up retailers including Walmart, opening the door to commissions on purchases. For the first time, there is a visible path to revenue beyond advertising.

A sharp rise in the share price, though, proves nothing about whether Muse will work. Prices capture mood and expectation, not results. A download is not a loyal user, and a loyal user is not a paying one. The early warning signs are concrete: Amazon has blocked Muse from its site, accusing it of browsing without identifying itself and of storing customers' login details, and on September 25 a New Mexico jury found that Meta had misled the state's residents. An agent that logs into people's accounts and spends their money runs on trust, which has long been Meta's weak spot.

3. Micron: from losses to paying down debt in three years

In the quarter to the end of May, Micron booked $41.5 billion of revenue, up from $9.3 billion a year earlier, at an 84.6% gross margin, and generated $18.3 billion of adjusted free cash flow. Over the first nine months of its fiscal year it repaid $9.4 billion of debt, taking long-term borrowings from $14.0 billion to $5.1 billion, and bought back $650 million of its own shares. As a business that pays its own way, Micron right now is hard to fault.

Micron reports its August quarter this Wednesday, September 30, against guidance of $50 billion in revenue, give or take $1 billion, and a gross margin of about 86%. Hitting that would mean another record. In a cyclical industry, though, a record is no reason to relax.

Rewind three years to fiscal 2023, and Micron's gross margin was negative 9%, with a $5.8 billion loss for the year. Minus 9% to plus 85% in three years: that is the memory cycle in a single line. High prices bring new factories; new factories bring lower prices, and prices usually fall faster than they rose. Management argues that its multi-year supply agreements with major customers will make results steadier and more predictable. That is a genuine hedge, but not one yet tested by a full downturn.

4. One hurdle, two very different answers

Line the two companies up against the same 5% bar, and almost every first impression turns out to need a second look.

Where the cash comes from, and where it goes

Meta: operating cash goes almost entirely into data centers, new debt covers the shortfall, and buybacks are on hold.

Micron: operating cash far outstrips what it needs to invest, leaving plenty to repay debt and buy back shares.

How much of the cost is already in the profits

Meta: very little of it so far. Data-center spending goes onto the balance sheet as an asset and hits earnings only gradually, as depreciation. Net property and equipment has grown from $176.4 billion to $225.7 billion in six months, and quarterly depreciation of $6.36 billion is already about 46% higher than a year ago. The bulk of that bill is still to come.

Micron: an 85% gross margin, rare in the industry's history, three years after it was below zero.

Put simply, both companies may be earning more today than they will on average over time: Meta because much of its cost has yet to arrive, Micron because prices are at a peak.

What the P/E is really telling you

Meta: about 28 times, a premium to the market. If revenue fails to keep pace with the depreciation on its way, today's earnings will flatter the future. If AI and Muse open up new income, today's price will look justified.

Micron: on peak earnings, the multiple will look cheap. As Peter Lynch warned, a cyclical stock tends to look cheapest just when its earnings are highest, which is also when it is most dangerous.

Where the risk sits

Meta: in the future. Will money borrowed at around 6% earn more than it costs?

Micron: in the cycle. When prices turn, how much of today's profit will survive?

Meta's open question is whether the investment will pay off. Micron's is how long the profits can last. One is a bet on the future, the other a business at the top of its cycle, and the 5% hurdle is no kinder to either.

In his 2007 letter to shareholders, Warren Buffett described three kinds of business: the great, which grows without needing much more capital; the good, which needs reinvestment but earns healthy returns on it; and the gruesome, which grows fast, swallows capital and earns little. For a decade Meta has been a textbook example of the first kind. Will this spending wave keep it there, or shift it into the second? That question is genuinely open. If AI sharpens its advertising and Muse creates new revenue, the spending could widen Meta's moat. If the returns are slow in coming, Meta starts to look like a business that must keep feeding in capital to grow. Micron, by contrast, lives in a capital-hungry industry by nature, and its returns depend largely on where the cycle stands. Neither is necessarily a poor investment, but both now need a stronger case for their price than they did when money was cheap.

5. Three questions for any stock after 5%

Who is paying for the growth? The company's own cash, or its lenders? With money priced again, borrowed growth eventually shows up as a cost. But self-funding should be judged across a whole cycle: Micron's cash today and its losses three years ago come from the same business.

Are today's profits normal, or a high? Meta's don't yet carry the full weight of its depreciation; Micron's may be riding a cyclical peak. Neither should simply be projected forward. What counts is average earning power through the cycle.

How much growth is already in the price? When a Treasury held to maturity pays 5% and a stock yields 3–4% in earnings, growth has to close the gap. How much of that growth rests on evidence, and how much on hope?

It cuts the other way, too. A lower valuation brought on by higher rates is not automatically a margin of safety. A falling price may be undoing past optimism, or it may be signalling that the long-term outlook has genuinely worsened. Telling the two apart takes work, one company at a time.

For investors whose first aim is to preserve wealth, none of this is bad news. When money was cheap, nearly everything rose together and discipline looked optional. At 5%, the rewards go back to those who understand cash flow, do the arithmetic on price, and have the patience to wait.

A Treasury offers a certain 5%; a stock offers uncertain growth. Choosing between them takes arithmetic, not courage, and a real understanding of the business.
