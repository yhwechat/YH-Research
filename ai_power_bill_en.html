AI’s Power Bill: Who Collects the Toll, and Who Fronts the Cash

By Yong Hui Research

IN BRIEF

This week Google signed up for two decades of nuclear power. That artificial intelligence runs on electricity is no longer news. The more useful question is where the money ends up. One stream of demand passes through three different businesses: the companies that make the equipment, the companies that sell the power and the regulated utilities that build the system. Each leaves its shareholders with a different bargain. This note asks who, on AI’s power bill, collects the toll and who fronts the cash.

1. A twenty-year contract

On October 6 Google and Constellation Energy (Nasdaq: CEG), America’s largest operator of nuclear plants, announced two agreements.

The first runs for 20 years and covers 890 megawatts of new nuclear capacity. No reactor will be built. Constellation will upgrade 11 existing units in Illinois, Pennsylvania and New Jersey so that they produce more power, at a cost of more than $4.3 billion, with the first of that power due in 2028. The second runs for 15 years and covers 2,700 megawatts from plants already in service. The 3,590 megawatts in the headlines is the two added together.

The plants sit on PJM, the country’s largest regional grid. Its managers have proposed that new data centers either bring their own power supply or accept being cut back when demand peaks. The proposal still needs federal approval and is not yet a rule, but the two companies described their deal as a response to it.

Any business owner will recognize what Google has bought: certainty over a critical input, much as a manufacturer might lock in a key supplier for twenty years. Constellation’s side is harder to judge. It must put up more than $4.3 billion first, and in exchange it gets a dependable customer for two decades. The price Google will pay has not been disclosed. The outlay is public and the selling price is not, so nobody outside the two companies can work out the return. That is the largest unknown in the deal.

It is also the first lesson. A large contract in a headline describes demand. It says nothing about returns.

2. One stream of demand, three businesses

Follow the money downstream and it passes through at least three kinds of company. They serve the same customers and keep very different books.

The equipment maker: GE Vernova (NYSE: GEV)

Spun out of General Electric, GE Vernova makes gas turbines and grid equipment. A power producer that wants to expand has to join its queue. At the end of the second quarter the company held firm orders for 53 gigawatts of gas-power equipment and reservations for production slots covering another 63, or 116 gigawatts in all. (A gigawatt is 1,000 megawatts.) It expects its factories to reach an annual rate of 20 gigawatts in the third quarter of this year, with 24 planned for 2028 and 30 targeted for 2030. Set orders and reservations against that rate and roughly six years of output is spoken for. This is a rough, static comparison and not a delivery schedule from the company; capacity is still rising.

Scarcity brings pricing power, and the company says its order growth reflects higher volumes and higher prices alike. Two caveats apply. A reservation holds a customer’s place in line and becomes an order only later, and an order is not yet a profit. In the second quarter GE Vernova earned $649 million on revenue of $11.1 billion, a net margin of 5.8%, and its wind business still lost money. Delays, cancellations and cost overruns can each shrink what an order book is worth.

The test: will the additional profit and cash flow justify the price investors pay today? Watch how fast reservations convert into orders, along with margins and free cash flow.

The power seller: Constellation Energy

Working nuclear plants have become scarce assets. A new one takes many years to build, whereas an upgrade can draw more power from an existing reactor fairly quickly, and long contracts secure revenue for a decade and more. A long contract makes revenue easier to see. It does not guarantee a profit.

The risks are plain enough. The money goes in first. Whether the upgrades finish on time and on budget, what power fetches outside the contracts and whether regulators step in will all shape the final account. PJM’s proposal shows the pattern: when data centers and households compete for the same grid, regulators do not stand aside.

The test: after capital spending and operating costs, how much free cash flow reaches shareholders? Watch the length and pricing of contracts, the actual cost of the upgrades and free cash flow.

The regulated utility: American Electric Power (Nasdaq: AEP)

Regulated utilities build the plants and string the lines that carry power to customers. A research arm of S&P Global expects America’s energy utilities to spend about $1.3 trillion on capital projects between 2026 and 2030.

American Electric Power shows what that means for one company. In May it raised its capital plan for 2026 to 2030 from $72 billion to $78 billion, or about $15.6 billion a year. Its operating earnings in 2025 were $3.19 billion. Accounting profit is not the same as cash on hand, but the gap makes the point: spending on this scale has to be funded by operating cash flow together with borrowing and new equity. That is what we mean by fronting the cash.

Fronting the cash does not mean losing it. The business resembles a licensed toll road. Regulators let approved investment join the asset base on which the utility charges customers an authorized return, so the more it invests, the larger the base that earns. In late July the company said its contracted new load through 2030 had reached 69 gigawatts, up from 56 at the start of the year. Contracted is not the same as consumed, however. Whether that load arrives on schedule depends on the terms of the agreements, the progress of customers’ projects and how much power they end up using. The company expects operating earnings to grow by 7% to 9% a year over the long term.

The danger is that three things fall out of step: the pace of spending, the cost of financing and the return regulators allow. The ten-year Treasury yielded 5.24% on October 9, so borrowing to build costs more, and allowed returns tend to adjust slowly. Nor can bills rise without limit. By S&P Global’s count, American utilities asked for $22.06 billion of rate increases in 2025, the most since its records began.

The test: once the new debt and new shares are counted, do earnings per share still grow? Watch the mix and cost of financing, the returns regulators allow and growth in the share count.

3. Strong demand is not the same as strong returns

The power industry last enjoyed certain demand and full order books a little over twenty years ago. After America opened its electricity markets in the late 1990s, a group of independent producers raced to build gas-fired plants. Calpine was the most aggressive, and it expanded largely on borrowed money. By early 2002 power prices had fallen and credit had tightened, and it cancelled orders for 34 gas turbines from General Electric, the business that is now GE Vernova. In December 2005 Calpine, by then America’s largest independent power producer, filed for bankruptcy protection with about $22 billion of debt and plants running at roughly 45% of capacity.

The plants went on generating, and the company emerged from reorganization in 2008. The shareholders who had paid for the build-out lost heavily. This January Calpine became part of Constellation.

The present differs from that episode in ways that matter, and the history is a warning, not a forecast. Most of those plants had no long-term buyer; they sold into the spot market and carried heavy debts. Today’s contracts run for 15 to 20 years, the buyers are companies as cash-rich as Google, and the utilities’ new load is backed by signed agreements. One lesson carries over. Real demand does not mean that shareholders at every link make money. Returns depend on how much capital goes in and on the price paid for the shares.

Price is the open question now. From the end of 2025 to the close on October 9, before dividends, GE Vernova’s shares rose by about 54% and Constellation’s fell by about 16%, a figure that includes a sharp rise on the day the Google deal was announced. Both companies face the same demand. The market’s popular explanation is that an equipment maker’s orders are visible while a generator awaits regulatory rulings. But the two began the year at different valuations, with different earnings expectations and different mixes of business, and the gap between their share prices does not show which is the better business.

It shows only this. A large rise means that more growth which has yet to happen is already in the price. A large fall does not make a share cheap, because the fall may reflect a real change in long-term prospects.

4. What this means for investors in US equities

Electricity is among the most tangible constraints on the AI build-out. Twenty-year contracts, equipment orders stretching years ahead and contracted load are real business, and there may be good investments at each of the three links. Capital and price are what separate them.

Nor will the bottleneck stay as it is. New generation, new transmission lines and large customers supplying their own power will ease the shortage over time. Projecting today’s tightness forward as lasting pricing power deserves the same caution.

Faced with any company said to benefit from AI’s appetite for power, we ask three questions first. They are the ones an owner would ask of any business.

What does each additional dollar invested earn? Growth in revenue is not growth in profit, and growth in profit is not growth in cash flow.

Where does the money come from? From the company’s own cash flow and its customers’ long contracts, or from repeated borrowing and share issues?

How much good news that has not yet happened is already in today’s price?

None of these can be answered from a headline. The answers are in the filings, the contracts and the regulatory dockets. We would not buy a company merely because it sits in the AI power chain. Growth becomes an attractive return only when durable new cash flow, a sound balance sheet and a purchase price that leaves room for error come together. The work of understanding the three businesses has to be done in advance, so that there is time to act when the price is right.

How much electricity AI will need matters less than which companies can turn that demand into lasting returns for shareholders, and what we must pay today to share in them.
